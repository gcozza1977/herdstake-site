What happened

Easterday Ranches, a large Washington cattle operation, agreed to buy and feed cattle for Tyson Foods and a second company, which advanced the cost of buying and raising them. According to the U.S. Department of Justice, from about 2016 to November 2020 the ranch's owner submitted, and had others submit, false invoices for cattle that were never bought. The two companies paid more than $244 million for roughly 265,000 head that did not exist.

It came apart after Tyson began looking into the cattle in the ranch's feedlots at the end of 2020. The owner pleaded guilty to wire fraud in March 2021 and in October 2022 was sentenced to 11 years in federal prison and ordered to pay $244 million in restitution. Easterday Ranches and a related company went into bankruptcy.

What caught it

Someone went to look: Tyson's own review of the cattle actually on hand.

Which check would have caught it sooner

Advance-funded feeding is credit by another name. The funder pays for cattle it never sees, exactly the position of a lender with livestock collateral.

The control is the same one a lender needs: an independent count, or a dated image of the lots, set against the head being billed. Over four years, any regular check of that kind would have found far fewer cattle in the pens than on the invoices.
